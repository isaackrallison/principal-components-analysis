Squash the data flat, rebuild it, and keep the version that loses the least.

Principal components analysis takes points in \(D\) dimensions and describes each one with only \(L\ll D\) numbers. The encoder is \(\mathbf z=\mathbf W^\top\mathbf x\), the decoder is \(\hat{\mathbf x}=\mathbf W\mathbf z\), and \(\mathbf W\) is chosen so that \(\hat{\mathbf x}\) lands as close to \(\mathbf x\) as possible. The best \(\mathbf W\) turns out to be the top \(L\) eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.

You will turn a projection line by hand until the error is smallest (20.1), compress 256-pixel images of the digit 9 down to two numbers and look at their eigen-images (20.1.1), see units mislead PCA (20.1.3.1), compute PCA from the small Gram matrix when \(D>N\) (20.1.3.2), compute it again with an exact and a randomized SVD (20.1.3.3), and then pick \(L\) using reconstruction error, scree plots and the profile likelihood (20.1.4). §20.1

PCA: project down, decode back up, measure what got lost

PCA looks for a linear, orthogonal map from \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^D\) to \(\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^L\). Encode with \(\mathbf z=\mathbf W^\top\mathbf x\), decode with \(\hat{\mathbf x}=\mathbf W\mathbf z\), and score \(\mathbf W\) by the average squared distance between \(\mathbf x\) and \(\hat{\mathbf x}\), the reconstruction error or distortion (Eq. 20.1).

The minimizer is \(\hat{\mathbf W}=\mathbf U_L\), the \(L\) eigenvectors of the empirical covariance \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) with the largest eigenvalues (Eq. 20.2). The Play box shows the case \(D=2\), \(L=1\) (Fig. 20.1): one line, and every point is replaced by its shadow on that line.

Explain itAt your level

Imagine a bunch of kids standing around a playground, and you have to describe where each kid is using just one number.

You could paint one straight line across the playground and say how far along the line each kid is. Every kid walks straight over to the line, and you write down where they land. Some kids have to walk a long way to reach it, and that walk is the information you lost.

Where should the line go? If the kids are spread out in a long stripe, paint the line along the stripe. Then nobody has to walk far. A line going the other way makes almost everyone walk a long way.

PCA is a rule for finding the line (or flat sheet) that makes the total walking as small as possible. Turn the line in the Play box and watch the orange walking paths get shorter and longer.

Each blue dot is a data point with two numbers, like (height, arm span). We want to describe it with one number. Pick a direction (a line through the average point). Drop each dot straight onto the line at a right angle. Its position along the line is its new single number \(z\).

To go back, put a dot at position \(z\) on the line. That's the reconstruction (red ring). The orange segment between the real dot and its ring is the error. Square each error, average them, and you get the score \(\mathcal L\).

Turn the line with the slider and the error curve on the right moves. Its lowest point is the first principal component. It's also where the red rings are most spread out along the line. Lose less, keep more spread: those are the same goal, because by Pythagoras (spread along the line)² + (error)² is the same total for every direction.

Center the data so \(\bar{\mathbf x}=\mathbf 0\). For a unit vector \(\mathbf w\), encode \(z_n=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n\) and decode \(\hat{\mathbf x}_n=z_n\mathbf w\). The distortion is \[\mathcal L(\mathbf w)=\frac1N\sum_n\|\mathbf x_n-z_n\mathbf w\|^2=\frac1N\sum_n\|\mathbf x_n\|^2-\mathbf w^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w.\] The first term doesn't depend on \(\mathbf w\), so minimizing distortion means maximizing \(\mathbf w^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w\), which is the variance of the projections.

With the constraint \(\|\mathbf w\|=1\), the Lagrangian gives \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w=\lambda\mathbf w\), so \(\mathbf w\) is an eigenvector, and \(\mathbf w^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w=\lambda\). Take the largest. For \(L>1\), each new direction is orthogonal to the earlier ones and is the next eigenvector, so \(\hat{\mathbf W}=\mathbf U_L\).

In the Play box, the right panel plots \(\mathcal L(\theta)\) and \(\mathbb V[z](\theta)\) for \(\mathbf w=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). They always add to \(\operatorname{tr}\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}=\lambda_1+\lambda_2\). The minimum of \(\mathcal L\) is at the angle of \(\mathbf u_1\), and its value is \(\lambda_2\).

PCA is the best rank-\(L\) linear autoencoder with tied, orthonormal weights. By Eckart–Young–Mirsky, \(\mathbf U_L\mathbf U_L^\top\mathbf X_c\) is the best rank-\(L\) approximation of the centered data in both Frobenius and spectral norm, so the orthonormality constraint costs nothing. An unconstrained linear autoencoder \(\hat{\mathbf x}=\mathbf D\mathbf E\mathbf x\) trained on squared error reaches the same subspace but not necessarily the same basis (any \(\mathbf W\mathbf R\) with invertible \(\mathbf R\) spans it).

The solution is unique only up to sign, and only up to rotation within an eigenspace when eigenvalues tie. Set the elongation slider to 1 in the Play box: \(\lambda_1\approx\lambda_2\), the error curve goes nearly flat, and the "first PC" jumps around between resamples. In high dimensions this instability shows up when the eigengap \(\lambda_L-\lambda_{L+1}\) is small (Davis–Kahan bounds the subspace angle by the perturbation norm divided by the gap).

Two interpretations sit behind the same eigenproblem: minimum distortion (Eq. 20.1) and maximum projected variance (Eq. 20.16). The book prefers the first because it generalizes, to kernel PCA, to autoencoders and to the probabilistic PCA likelihood of §20.2, where the MLE of \(\mathbf W\) spans the same \(\mathbf U_L\).

Math, slowlyFrom the objective to an eigenvector

The objective (Eqs. 20.1–20.2)

  1. Distortion: \(\displaystyle \mathcal L(\mathbf W)\triangleq\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\big\|\mathbf x_n-\operatorname{decode}(\operatorname{encode}(\mathbf x_n;\mathbf W);\mathbf W)\big\|_2^2\), with \(\operatorname{encode}(\mathbf x)=\mathbf W^\top\mathbf x\) and \(\operatorname{decode}(\mathbf z)=\mathbf W\mathbf z\). Both maps are linear, and \(\mathbf W\) is \(D\times L\) with orthonormal columns.
  2. Empirical covariance: \(\displaystyle \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}=\frac1N\sum_n(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})^\top=\frac1N\mathbf X_c^\top\mathbf X_c\), where \(\mathbf X_c\) is the centered \(N\times D\) design matrix.
  3. Claim: \(\hat{\mathbf W}=\mathbf U_L\), the eigenvectors of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) with the \(L\) largest eigenvalues.

Why (the 1-D case, condensed from §20.1.2)

  1. Error for one point: \(\|\mathbf x_n-z_{n}\mathbf w\|^2=\mathbf x_n^\top\mathbf x_n-2z_{n}\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n+z_{n}^2\), using \(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w=1\).
  2. Best code: \(\partial/\partial z_n=0\Rightarrow z_n=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_n\). Orthogonal projection. In the plot, each orange residual meets the line at a right angle.
  3. Plug back in: \(\mathcal L(\mathbf w)=\text{const}-\frac1N\sum_nz_n^2=\text{const}-\mathbf w^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w\). The const is \(\operatorname{tr}\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\), which is the "𝓛 + 𝕍" stat in the Play box.
  4. Maximize \(\mathbf w^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w-\lambda(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w-1)\): \(\;2\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w-2\lambda\mathbf w=0\Rightarrow\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w=\lambda\mathbf w\).
  5. Then \(\mathbf w^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf w=\lambda\), so choose the largest eigenvalue. The leftover error is \(\mathcal L(\mathbf u_1)=\lambda_2\) in 2-D, and \(\sum_{j>L}\lambda_j\) in general (Eq. 20.26).
Power iteration multiplies by \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) and renormalizes. Each step shrinks the \(\mathbf u_2\) component relative to \(\mathbf u_1\) by a factor \(\lambda_2/\lambda_1\), so it converges in a few clicks for elongated data and slowly when the elongation is near 1.

§20.1.1

Examples: 256 pixels, two numbers, and eigen-images

Fig. 20.2 projects MNIST images of the digit 9 to 2-D. The images have \(28\times28=784\) pixels, but neighboring pixels are strongly correlated and most 9s look alike, so the number of effective degrees of freedom is far smaller. Fig. 20.3 does the same for 64×64 faces. The columns of \(\mathbf W\) can be shown as images, and for faces they are called eigenfaces.

MNIST and Olivetti are too big to ship in one page, so this lab generates its own 16×16 handwritten-style 9s (\(D=256\)). Each one is drawn with a random slant, stroke thickness, loop size, tail curl and shift, plus pixel noise. PCA is fit on 300 training images.

Explain itAt your level

A picture of a number 9 is made of 256 little squares, each light or dark. That's a lot of numbers!

But all the 9s look kind of alike. Some lean to the left, some lean to the right. Some are drawn with a fat marker, some with a thin pencil. If you know "how much it leans" and "how fat the marker was", you can almost draw the whole picture again.

PCA finds those few "knobs" by itself, just by looking at lots of 9s. Nobody tells it about leaning or marker size. In the Play box, the 9s on the left side lean one way and on the right side lean the other way, and going up or down they get thinner or fatter.

A 16×16 image is a point in a 256-dimensional space: one coordinate per pixel. That sounds hopeless to picture, but real images only fill a tiny corner of that space. Random pixels look like TV static. 9s are much more organized.

PCA finds the directions in pixel space along which the 9s differ the most. Each direction is itself a 256-pixel pattern, so we can draw it as an image (blue pixels get added, red pixels get subtracted). Any 9 is then roughly the average 9 + a bit of pattern 1 + a bit of pattern 2 + …

The scatter plot gives each image just two numbers, how much of pattern 1 and pattern 2 it contains. Click around the scatter to see the image those two numbers build. Then use the sliders under the eigen-images to add or subtract each pattern from the average.

Stack the 300 centered training images as rows of \(\mathbf X_c\in\mathbb R^{300\times256}\), take the top eigenvectors of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}=\frac1N\mathbf X_c^\top\mathbf X_c\), and plot \(\mathbf z_n=\mathbf W^\top(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})\in\mathbb R^2\). As in Fig. 20.2, the grid lines sit at the 5, 25, 50, 75 and 95% quantiles of each coordinate, and the circled points are the images nearest each grid vertex.

The latent axes have no names, so we interpret them by looking at the examples along each one. Here the horizontal axis mostly tracks slant and left-right position, and the vertical axis tracks stroke thickness (the second eigen-image is almost entirely one sign: "more ink everywhere on the stroke"). That matches what the book reports for real MNIST 9s.

Use \(\mathbf z=\mathbf W^\top\mathbf x\) as input to a nearest-neighbor classifier and you get the eigenfaces recognizer of [Tur13]. It is faster than working with raw pixels, and more reliable [MWP98], because the discarded directions are mostly noise.

Eigen-images are global, dense and signed. Each basis image touches every pixel and cancels the others, which makes them hard to read beyond the first few. Methods like NMF (nonnegative parts), sparse PCA and ICA trade some reconstruction error for more interpretable bases.

The quantile grid in Fig. 20.2 is a cheap way to look at a latent space. It avoids the tails, where projected points are rare and nearest-neighbor exemplars would be misleading. With real MNIST you'll also see that PC1 mixes slant with stroke curvature, because PCA directions are eigenvectors, not causes. Independent generative factors map to orthogonal PCs only if they contribute uncorrelated, linearly separable pixel changes with distinct variances.

Eigenfaces work for recognition partly because the top PCs of face images are dominated by lighting (the book's PC1). Fisherfaces (LDA after PCA) were introduced to discount exactly that within-class variation. With a modern CNN embedding, PCA is still used for whitening and compressing the features before retrieval.

Math, slowlyImages as vectors

  1. Flatten each \(16\times16\) image into \(\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^{256}\) (row by row). Pixel order doesn't matter to PCA, as long as it's the same for every image.
  2. Center: \(\bar{\mathbf x}=\frac1N\sum_n\mathbf x_n\) is itself an image, "the average 9" (first tile in the basis row).
  3. Eigendecompose the \(256\times256\) matrix \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\). Each eigenvector \(\mathbf w_k\in\mathbb R^{256}\) reshapes into a \(16\times16\) "eigen-image". It is a unit vector, so its pixels have both signs.
  4. Encode: \(z_{nk}=\mathbf w_k^\top(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})\), how much of pattern \(k\) image \(n\) contains.
  5. Decode with \(L\) components: \(\hat{\mathbf x}_n=\bar{\mathbf x}+\sum_{k=1}^Lz_{nk}\mathbf w_k\). Storage per image falls from 256 numbers to \(L\), plus the shared \(\bar{\mathbf x}\) and \(\mathbf W\).
  6. Variance of coordinate \(k\) across the data is \(\lambda_k\), so \(z_k/\sqrt{\lambda_k}\) is in standard-deviation units. That's what the sliders use, and why ±2.5 stays within realistic 9s.

§20.1.3.1

Covariance vs correlation: don't let the units choose the axes

PCA on the covariance matrix finds directions of large variance, but variance depends on units. Measure weight in ounces instead of kilograms and its variance grows by \(35^2\approx1200\). PCA will then call weight the "main direction", even though nothing about the people changed. Fig. 20.5 shows this on height/weight data.

The fix is to standardize each feature to mean 0 and variance 1 first. That is the same as running PCA on the correlation matrix, which doesn't change when you rescale a feature.

Explain itAt your level

Say you measure your friends' heights in meters and their weights in grams.

Heights are numbers like 1.6 and 1.8, which are close together. Weights are numbers like 60,000 and 80,000, which are super far apart. If you only look at the numbers, weight seems way more "spread out", so PCA thinks weight is the only thing that matters.

But that's just because grams are tiny! Measure weight in kilograms and suddenly it's 60 and 80. To be fair, we first squish every measurement so it has the same amount of spread. Then the units can't trick us.

The variance of a feature is in squared units. Change pounds to ounces (×16) and the variance goes up 256×. PCA chases variance, so its first direction swings toward whichever feature has the biggest numbers.

Standardizing means: for each feature, subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation. Now every feature has spread 1 and no units at all, so changing units does nothing.

In the Play box, switch the units on the left plot and watch PC1 swing between "almost pure height" and "almost pure weight". The standardized plot on the right doesn't move. For two features, its PC1 is always the 45° diagonal, (1, 1)/√2, or the other diagonal when the correlation is negative.

Let \(\mathbf S=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_D)\) hold the feature standard deviations. Standardized data is \(\tilde{\mathbf X}=\mathbf X_c\mathbf S^{-1}\), and its covariance is the correlation matrix \(\mathbf R=\mathbf S^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf S^{-1}\).

A change of units is \(\mathbf X\to\mathbf X\mathbf A\) with \(\mathbf A\) diagonal. Then \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\to\mathbf A\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf A\), and its eigenvectors change. But \(\mathbf S\to\mathbf A\mathbf S\), so \(\mathbf R\) doesn't change. PCA on \(\mathbf R\) is unit-invariant.

With \(D=2\), \(\mathbf R=\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\), whose eigenvectors are always \((1,\pm1)/\sqrt2\) with eigenvalues \(1\pm\rho\). Only the share of variance (\((1+|\rho|)/2\)) depends on the data.

Standardizing isn't always right. If features share units and their scale carries meaning (pixel intensities, spectra, gene expression on one assay), standardizing inflates near-constant noisy features to unit variance and can bury the signal. That's why image PCA is usually run on the covariance.

Standardizing makes PCA invariant to diagonal rescaling only. It is not invariant to general linear maps: rotate or mix the features and the answer changes. Full whitening (\(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1/2}\)) gives an isotropic covariance and destroys all PCA structure. Other choices sit between the two: Pareto scaling (divide by \(\sqrt{s_j}\)), robust scaling with the MAD, or weighting features by domain priors.

The scale problem also affects every \(\ell_2\)-based method (k-means, kNN, RBF kernels). Here it comes up as "which directions get kept". In probabilistic terms, PPCA assumes isotropic noise \(\sigma^2\mathbf I\), which only makes sense when features are on comparable scales. Factor analysis (§20.2) gives each feature its own noise variance and is invariant to rescaling.

Math, slowlyWhy the correlation matrix ignores units

  1. Change units by scaling feature \(j\) by \(a_j\) (inches → cm: \(a=2.54\)). In matrix form \(\mathbf X\to\mathbf X\mathbf A\), \(\mathbf A=\operatorname{diag}(a_j)\).
  2. Covariance becomes \(\mathbf A\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\mathbf A\). Entry \((i,j)\) is multiplied by \(a_ia_j\), so a large \(a_j\) inflates feature \(j\)'s diagonal entry by \(a_j^2\). That's the swing you see on the left.
  3. Standard deviations become \(a_js_j\). The correlation \(R_{ij}=\hat\Sigma_{ij}/(s_is_j)\) becomes \(a_ia_j\hat\Sigma_{ij}/(a_is_ia_js_j)=R_{ij}\). Unchanged.
  4. Standardizing each column, \(\tilde x_{nj}=(x_{nj}-\bar x_j)/s_j\), gives data whose covariance is exactly \(\mathbf R\). So "standardize, then PCA" = "PCA on \(\mathbf R\)".
  5. To read the result in original units, map a direction back through \(\mathbf S\). \(\mathbf u\) in standardized space corresponds to \(\mathbf S\mathbf u\) (unnormalized) in raw space.
Tick "equal axis scales": with a big unit mismatch, the raw cloud becomes a thin stripe along one axis, and that stripe is PC1. Without equal scales, the raw PC1 and PC2 may not look perpendicular, because the axes are stretched differently.

§20.1.3.2

High-dimensional data: when D > N, eigendecompose the small matrix

The covariance \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) is \(D\times D\), and eigendecomposing it costs \(O(D^3)\). With 300 face images of 4096 pixels each, that's a 4096×4096 problem. The Gram matrix \(\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\) is only \(N\times N\) (300×300) and has the same nonzero eigenvalues. Its eigenvectors map to those of \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) with one multiplication and a rescale (Eqs. 20.21–20.22). This is also the step that makes kernel PCA possible (§20.4.6).

Explain itAt your level

Imagine comparing 10 kids, and for each kid you have 1,000 facts.

One way is to compare every fact with every other fact: a million comparisons. Another way is to compare every kid with every other kid: only 100 comparisons. When there are way more facts than kids, comparing kids is much faster, and it turns out you can get the same answer from it.

\(\mathbf X\) is a table with \(N\) rows (examples) and \(D\) columns (features). There are two "square" tables you can build from it. \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) (\(D\times D\)) compares features with features. \(\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\) (\(N\times N\)) compares examples with examples: entry \((i,j)\) is the dot product of example \(i\) with example \(j\).

The cost of finding eigenvectors grows like the size cubed. When \(D\) is much bigger than \(N\), the small table is far cheaper. And they have the same important eigenvalues, so you lose nothing. In the Play box, push \(D\) up and compare the two timings.

Let \(\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda\). Multiply on the left by \(\mathbf X^\top\): \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U)=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U)\boldsymbol\Lambda\). So the columns of \(\mathbf V=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U\) are eigenvectors of \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) with the same eigenvalues (Eq. 20.21).

They have the wrong length: \(\|\mathbf v_j\|^2=\mathbf u_j^\top\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\mathbf u_j=\lambda_j\). Normalize: \(\mathbf V=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1/2}\) (Eq. 20.22). The cost drops from \(O(D^3)\) to \(O(N^2D+N^3)\).

A centered \(\mathbf X\) has rank at most \(\min(N-1,D)\), so at most \(N-1\) eigenvalues are nonzero. You can't normalize the zero ones, and you don't need to, because they carry no variance.

This is the SVD seen from both sides. If \(\mathbf X=\mathbf U\mathbf S\mathbf V^\top\), then \(\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top=\mathbf U\mathbf S^2\mathbf U^\top\) and \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X=\mathbf V\mathbf S^2\mathbf V^\top\), with \(\mathbf V=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U\mathbf S^{-1}\). The Gram route only touches \(\mathbf X\) through inner products \(\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j\). Swap those for \(\mathcal K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\) and center in feature space, and you have kernel PCA.

Numerically, forming either product squares the condition number, so small singular values lose about half their significant digits. For accuracy, prefer a thin SVD of \(\mathbf X\) itself (\(O(ND\min(N,D))\)). With tiny \(\lambda_j\), \(\boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1/2}\) amplifies rounding error, which you can watch in the "max |cos| error" stat if you set \(N\) close to \(D\) with low rank.

When \(N\) and \(D\) are both huge, neither square matrix fits in memory. Then use iterative Lanczos/ARPACK, randomized SVD (next section) or streaming methods (Oja's rule, incremental PCA).

Math, slowlyEqs. 20.21–20.22

  1. Gram eigenproblem: \((\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top)\mathbf U=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda\), with \(\mathbf U^\top\mathbf U=\mathbf I\). \(N\times N\): cheap when \(N\ll D\).
  2. Left-multiply by \(\mathbf X^\top\): \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda\), i.e. \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)\mathbf V=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\) with \(\mathbf V=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U\). (20.21)
  3. Length: \(\|\mathbf v_j\|^2=\mathbf u_j^\top\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\mathbf u_j=\mathbf u_j^\top(\lambda_j\mathbf u_j)=\lambda_j\).
  4. Normalize: \(\mathbf V=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1/2}\). (20.22) Only for \(\lambda_j>0\). After centering there are at most \(N-1\) of them.
  5. Cost: forming \(\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\) is \(O(N^2D)\), its eigendecomposition is \(O(N^3)\), and \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf U\) is \(O(ND\,N)\). Compare \(O(ND^2+D^3)\) for route A.
The eigenvalues here are of \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\), not \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\). Divide by \(N\) to get the PCA variances. Eigenvectors don't care about the factor.

§20.1.3.3

PCA by SVD, exact and randomized

The thin SVD of the centered data, \(\mathbf X\approx\mathbf U_X\mathbf S_X\mathbf V_X^\top\), gives PCA directly. The right singular vectors are the principal directions, \(\mathbf V_X=\mathbf W\). The scores are \(\mathbf Z=\mathbf X\mathbf W=\mathbf U_X\mathbf S_X\) (Eq. 20.23). The reconstruction \(\hat{\mathbf X}=\mathbf Z\mathbf W^\top\) is the truncated SVD (Eq. 20.24). Eigenvalues and singular values are related by \(\lambda_k=s_k^2/N\).

For very large problems, a randomized SVD finds the top \(L\) components in \(O(NL^2)+O(L^3)\) time after a few passes over the data, instead of \(O(ND^2)+O(D^3)\) for an exact solver.

Explain itAt your level

There's more than one way to find the same treasure.

Before, we found the important directions by building a big comparison table first. The SVD skips the table and goes straight at the data. It's like taking a photo apart into a few simple layers that, stacked together, look almost like the photo. More layers, better photo.

The "randomized" trick is like shaking the data a few times in random directions and watching which way it wobbles most. It's a shortcut that works really well when you only want the top few layers.

Any table of numbers can be written as a sum of simple pieces, each one "a column pattern times a row pattern times a strength". That's the SVD. The strengths \(s_k\) come sorted from biggest to smallest. Keep the top \(L\) pieces and you have the best possible \(L\)-piece approximation of the table.

For centered data, the row patterns are exactly PCA's directions, and \(s_k^2/N\) is the variance along direction \(k\). In the Play box, the blocks show the shapes of the three matrices. The slider changes \(L\), and the 9s get rebuilt from \(L\) pieces.

From \(\mathbf X=\mathbf U\mathbf S\mathbf V^\top\): \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X=\mathbf V\mathbf S^2\mathbf V^\top\), so the right singular vectors are eigenvectors of \(N\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\) and \(\lambda_k=s_k^2/N\). Then \[\mathbf Z=\mathbf X\mathbf V_L=\mathbf U\mathbf S\mathbf V^\top\mathbf V_L=\mathbf U_L\mathbf S_L,\qquad\hat{\mathbf X}=\mathbf Z\mathbf V_L^\top=\mathbf U_L\mathbf S_L\mathbf V_L^\top.\]

Randomized SVD [HMT11]. (1) Draw a random \(D\times(L+p)\) matrix \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) and form \(\mathbf Y=\mathbf X\boldsymbol\Omega\), which samples the column space. (2) Optionally do \(q\) power iterations \(\mathbf Y\leftarrow\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\mathbf Y\) to sharpen the spectrum. (3) Orthonormalize: \(\mathbf Y=\mathbf Q\mathbf R\). (4) Do an exact SVD of the small \((L+p)\times D\) matrix \(\mathbf B=\mathbf Q^\top\mathbf X\). (5) Lift back: \(\mathbf U=\mathbf Q\tilde{\mathbf U}\).

Randomized range finding comes with an expected error bound: \(\mathbb E\|\mathbf X-\mathbf Q\mathbf Q^\top\mathbf X\|\le\big(1+\tfrac{4\sqrt{L+p}}{p-1}\sqrt{\min(N,D)}\big)\,s_{L+1}\), and \(q\) power iterations shrink the tail term by \((s_{L+1}/s_L)^{2q}\)-type factors. Oversampling \(p\approx5\)–10 and \(q\in\{1,2\}\) are standard (sklearn's svd_solver="randomized" uses \(p=10\) and \(q\) chosen by size). Re-orthonormalize between power steps or the columns collapse onto \(\mathbf u_1\).

The quoted \(O(NL^2)+O(L^3)\) counts the small factorizations. Each pass over the data also costs \(O(NDL)\), which dominates when \(D\) is large but is easy to parallelize and stream. For sparse \(\mathbf X\), don't center explicitly (that would densify it). Use implicit centering \(\mathbf X\boldsymbol\Omega-\mathbf 1\bar{\mathbf x}^\top\boldsymbol\Omega\) instead, as TruncatedSVD and LSA do.

Signs of singular vector pairs are arbitrary, so compare subspaces or use \(|\cos|\), as the table does. Use svd_flip-style conventions if you need reproducible signs across runs.

§20.1.4.1

Choosing L by reconstruction error, and why the test curve won't turn up

Define \(\mathcal L_L=\frac1{|\mathcal D|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal D}\|\mathbf x_n-\hat{\mathbf x}_n\|^2\), with \(\hat{\mathbf x}_n=\mathbf W\mathbf z_n+\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\mathbf z_n=\mathbf W^\top(\mathbf x_n-\boldsymbol\mu)\) (Eq. 20.25). On the training set it falls to 0 at \(L=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf X)\). On a test set, unlike supervised learning, it keeps falling, with no U-shape (Fig. 20.6). PCA isn't a proper generative model, so extra dimensions can only help it rebuild new data.

Explain itAt your level

How many knobs should we keep? More knobs always draw the picture better.

With 1 knob the 9s look blurry. With 10 they look good. With 256 knobs, one for every little square, you can copy any picture perfectly, even a brand-new one. So "how well does it copy?" never tells you to stop. That's a puzzle the next two sections try to solve.

In ordinary machine learning, adding complexity eventually makes test error go up (overfitting), and the bottom of that U tells you where to stop. PCA's test reconstruction error doesn't do that. It just keeps going down.

Why? A bigger \(L\) means a bigger flat "sheet" to drop points onto, and a bigger sheet is closer to every point, old or new. At \(L=D\) the sheet is the whole space, and every point is rebuilt exactly. Slide \(L\) in the Play box and compare the blue (train) and orange (test) curves.

Training error equals the sum of discarded eigenvalues, \(\mathcal L_L=\sum_{j>L}\lambda_j\) (Eq. 20.26), and the Play box checks this numerically. The test error uses the same \(\mathbf W\) and \(\boldsymbol\mu\) on held-out images. It's a little larger, because the training eigenvectors fit some noise, but it is still monotone non-increasing in \(L\). Projecting onto nested subspaces \(\mathcal S_1\subset\mathcal S_2\subset\cdots\) can only shrink the residual.

The book's remedy is to look at the shape of these curves (scree, elbow, profile likelihood) or to use a probabilistic model (PPCA, §20.2) whose held-out likelihood does penalize extra dimensions. The same problem shows up with K-means reconstruction error vs \(K\) (§21.3.7).

Squared reconstruction error is a projection residual, \(\|(\mathbf I-\mathbf P_L)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\|^2\), and \(\mathbf P_L\preceq\mathbf P_{L+1}\), so monotonicity holds for every point, not just on average. A held-out criterion needs a model that pays for capacity. Under PPCA, \(\log p(\mathbf x_{\text{test}})\) charges both for the noise variance \(\sigma^2\) (estimated as the mean of the discarded eigenvalues) and for the volume of \(\mathbf C=\mathbf W\mathbf W^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf I\), so it peaks at some finite \(L\).

Other principled choices: Minka's Bayesian evidence for PPCA (Laplace approximation), cross-validation on the PPCA likelihood, and "missing-entry" CV (hold out random matrix entries, impute them with rank-\(L\) SVD and score the imputation). Missing-entry CV does give a U-shape, because the held-out entries aren't used to choose their own projection. Parallel analysis (compare λ with eigenvalues of column-permuted data) and the Marchenko–Pastur edge for isotropic noise are popular nonparametric thresholds.

§20.1.4.2

Scree plots and the fraction of variance explained

A scree plot shows the eigenvalues \(\lambda_j\) in decreasing order (Fig. 20.7a). Since \(\mathcal L_L=\sum_{j=L+1}^D\lambda_j\), it carries the same information as the training error curve. The fraction of variance explained \(F_L=\sum_{j\le L}\lambda_j/\sum_{j'\le L_{\max}}\lambda_{j'}\) (Eq. 20.27) accumulates it and rises toward 1 (Fig. 20.7b).

"Scree" is the pile of rubble at the foot of a cliff. The few big eigenvalues are the cliff and the long flat tail is the rubble.

Explain itAt your level

Line up the knobs from most important to least important and draw a bar for each.

The first few bars are really tall, like a cliff. Then they get short and stay short, like the little rocks at the bottom of a cliff. The tall ones are the knobs worth keeping. The short ones are mostly just wiggles and noise.

Each eigenvalue is the amount of spread (variance) along one direction. Sort them from biggest to smallest and plot them. That's the scree plot.

The running total, divided by the grand total, is the "fraction of variance explained". If the first 10 directions explain 90%, you can describe the data with 10 numbers and lose only 10% of the spread. A common rule of thumb is to keep enough components for 90% or 95%. Drag the threshold slider in the Play box to see what \(L\) each rule picks.

Two identities connect the plots: \(\mathcal L_L=\sum_{j>L}\lambda_j\) and \(F_L=1-\mathcal L_L/\mathcal L_0\) when \(L_{\max}=D\). The scree is the negative slope of the training error curve: \(\lambda_L=\mathcal L_{L-1}-\mathcal L_L\).

Common heuristics: keep the smallest \(L\) with \(F_L\ge\tau\) (often \(\tau=0.9\) or 0.95), keep \(\lambda_j\) above the mean eigenvalue (Kaiser's rule, which on standardized data means \(\lambda>1\)), or eyeball the elbow. Try the synthetic data with a known number of factors \(L^*\) and see which rule finds it.

Variance thresholds aren't consistent estimators of the true rank. With isotropic noise \(\sigma^2\) and \(D/N\to\gamma\), the noise eigenvalues of the sample covariance spread over the Marchenko–Pastur bulk \(\sigma^2(1\pm\sqrt\gamma)^2\), not around \(\sigma^2\). A signal spike \(\ell\) is only detectable if \(\ell>\sigma^2(1+\sqrt\gamma)\) (the BBP phase transition), and its sample eigenvalue is biased upward. Gavish–Donoho's optimal hard threshold \(\approx2.858\,\mathrm{median}(s)\) for square matrices comes from this.

\(F_L\) also depends on scaling (standardized or not) and on \(L_{\max}\) in Eq. 20.27. The book's Fig. 20.7b normalizes by the first 50 eigenvalues, so its 90% is relative to that truncated total. For supervised pipelines, the right \(L\) is whatever minimizes downstream validation loss, which can be much smaller or larger than any variance rule suggests.

§20.1.4.3

Profile likelihood: let a change-point model find the elbow

Eigenvalue curves often have a knee: fast decrease while \(L<L^*\), then slow once the model has captured the real structure. The profile likelihood of [ZG06] finds the knee automatically. For each split point \(L\), model the first \(L\) eigenvalues as i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(\mu_1,\sigma^2)\) and the rest as \(\mathcal N(\mu_2,\sigma^2)\), with a shared \(\sigma^2\). Fit by maximum likelihood, record \(\ell(L)\), and pick \(L^*=\arg\max_L\ell(L)\) (Eqs. 20.28–20.31, Fig. 20.8).

Explain itAt your level

Imagine a line of kids sorted from tallest to shortest, and you want to split them into a "tall group" and a "short group".

You try putting the split after the first kid, then after the second, then after the third, and so on. Each time you ask: "Does everybody look like they belong in their group?" The best split is where both groups look the most alike inside. For PCA, the "tall group" is the knobs worth keeping.

The scree plot usually looks like a few big values followed by many small ones. We'd like to cut between "big" and "small" automatically.

Try every cut position \(L\). For each one, compute the average of the left group and the average of the right group, then measure how tightly the values sit around their group's average, using one shared spread for both. Good cuts make both groups tight. The profile likelihood turns "tightness" into a score, and the highest score wins. In the Play box, drag \(L\) and watch the two dashed averages and the score.

For a split at \(L\), the MLEs are \(\mu_1(L)=\frac1L\sum_{k\le L}\lambda_k\), \(\mu_2(L)=\frac1{L_{\max}-L}\sum_{k>L}\lambda_k\), and \(\sigma^2(L)=\frac1{L_{\max}}\big[\sum_{k\le L}(\lambda_k-\mu_1)^2+\sum_{k>L}(\lambda_k-\mu_2)^2\big]\) (Eqs. 20.28–20.30). Plug them into the Gaussian log-likelihood (Eq. 20.31). Since \(\sigma^2\) is the MLE, \(\ell(L)=-\frac{L_{\max}}2\big(\log2\pi\sigma^2(L)+1\big)\). Maximizing \(\ell\) is the same as minimizing the pooled within-group variance.

The variance must be shared. With separate variances, a group of one or two values would get \(\hat\sigma^2\to0\) and an infinite likelihood. The i.i.d. assumption is obviously wrong (the λ's are sorted), but it's good enough to locate the change point.

This is one-dimensional 2-means (Jenks natural breaks), restricted to contiguous splits of a sorted sequence, so it is solved exactly by an \(O(L_{\max})\) scan with prefix sums. Its weaknesses follow from that. It's dominated by the largest eigenvalues in absolute terms, so a single huge \(\lambda_1\) can pull \(L^*\) to 1 even when several components matter. Running it on \(\log\lambda\) is a common robustification. Try the digits with and without the log toggle.

The book notes the same method applies to K-means distortion vs \(K\) (§21.3.7). Alternatives with a probabilistic reading are Minka's PPCA evidence, BIC on the PPCA likelihood, and the "gap statistic" style comparison with a null reference. For the spiked-covariance model, eigenvalues above the MP edge \(\sigma^2(1+\sqrt{D/N})^2\) are a principled count.
